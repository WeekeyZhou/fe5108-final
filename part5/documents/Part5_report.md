# Part 5 Portfolio Analytics

## 5A Factor composite definitions

MSCI averages book value/price, forward 12-month earnings/price and dividend-yield z-scores to define value. Growth uses long- and short-term forecast EPS growth, internal growth (ROE times earnings retention), and historical EPS and sales-per-share trends. Standard indexes double-weight long-term forecast growth. Value and growth are separate dimensions, so a security may have both characteristics and partial inclusion in both indexes. Small Cap omits long-term forecast growth. Banks and Diversified Financials omit sales growth, except Multi-Sector Holdings and Financial Exchanges & Data. Missing signals are excluded and remaining weights renormalized; share-class dividend differences can change internal growth. Economically, these exceptions accommodate differences in accounting relevance and data availability. Fama-French HML instead uses book-to-market alone: average small- and big-value returns minus average small- and big-growth returns. HML is a long-short spread; MSCI USA Value is a long-only, free-float-weighted large/mid-cap style index (MSCI, 2021, Sections 1.2, 2.1–2.3; French Data Library). MSCI's methodology evolved over the sample period: before May 2003, style classification was primarily based on price-to-book, whereas the subsequent framework incorporated multiple value and growth indicators (MSCI, 2021).

## 5B Identifying strategy factor exposures

Using all 343 months from January 1995 to July 2023, we regress gross USD MSCI Value total returns on an intercept, RF, Mkt-RF, HML and SMB, in decimal units. One index provides a time series, so the assignment's pooled-panel wording is implemented as time-series OLS. Conventional standard errors are used without Newey-West adjustment. Market exposure is 0.948, HML 0.322, and SMB -0.174, all significant at 1%. The positive value tilt and large-cap bias are as advertised (R² = 94.84%). RF exposure is 0.865; it is estimated rather than fixed at one (Table 5.1).

## 5C Enhancing factor performance

The intercept is -0.071% monthly, or -0.86% per year arithmetically (-85.5 basis points). It equals the mean return gap between MSCI Value and its intercept-excluding replication. With t = -0.906 and p = 0.366, it is not statistically significant; the annualized 95% interval is [-2.71%, 1.00%]. The estimate is economically unfavorable, but the uncertainty does not establish underperformance. The results provide no statistically significant evidence that MSCI's more elaborate value methodology generates additional returns beyond the selected Fama-French factor exposures. This does not establish that the methodology has no investment value.

## 5D Fund replication

We sum each same-month factor return times its estimated slope, excluding alpha. Correlation with MSCI Value is 0.974, indicating effective in-sample replication (Figure 5.1). Replication earns 10.73% annually versus 9.87%, with volatility of 14.87% versus 15.27% and Sharpe ratios of 0.576 versus 0.505 (Table 5.2). Arithmetic returns are monthly means times 12; volatility is sample monthly standard deviation times √12. Sharpe subtracts the aligned FF risk-free mean and divides by total-return volatility, following assignment footnote 2. The factor replication performs better in sample before implementation costs, although the fitted coefficients use the full historical sample and do not establish out-of-sample superiority. Selection still requires evaluating turnover and fees, shorting/financing and liquidity, and out-of-sample stability. These unconstrained loadings on academic spreads do not define an automatically tradable, fully invested portfolio.

## 5E Relative performance analysis

We reuse Part 1C's monthly-rebalanced average of Value, Minimum Volatility, Equal Weight and Momentum, restoring unrounded values from the original levels and reconciling the rounded CSV. Active returns subtract MSCI USA. The multi-factor portfolio earns 0.152% per year actively, with 3.39% tracking error and information ratio 0.045 (Table 5.3). The reward is small relative to benchmark risk despite the improved absolute Sharpe in Part 1C. Compounded relative wealth gains 9.08% over the full sample (Figure 5.2); this wealth ratio is distinct from annualized mean active return.

## 5F Risk decomposition

For each component, active-variance contribution equals 0.25 times its covariance with multi-factor active returns. Minimum Volatility contributes the most (34.1%), followed by Momentum (24.8%), Value (23.3%) and Equal Weight (17.9%). Contributions sum exactly to sample active variance within floating-point tolerance; all are positive here (Table 5.4; Figure 5.3). These allocate variance, not volatility. Minimum Volatility accounts for the largest share of active-return variance (34.1%), despite its defensive objective. This highlights the distinction between absolute volatility and benchmark-relative risk. It may be more suitable as a standalone defensive equity allocation for investors prioritizing absolute risk reduction than as a tightly benchmark-constrained strategy. Prolonged benchmark underperformance can trigger redemptions or manager termination, illustrating limits to arbitrage (Shleifer and Vishny, 1997). Suitability nevertheless depends on the investor's mandate and implementation costs.
